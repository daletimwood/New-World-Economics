About

New World Economics is a living research document tracking the structural shift from transactional to value-based commercial models in enterprise technology.

The question at the centre of this work (how value is created, captured, and shared in enterprise technology) does not yield to a single analytical pass. The argument develops gradually, through a process closer to gestation than to modelling: material accumulates, patterns surface, and the sections are rewritten as understanding changes.

Rilke, advising a young poet, wrote: 'Live the questions now. Perhaps then, someday far in the future, you will gradually, without even noticing it, live your way into the answer.' (Letters to a Young Poet, Letter Four, written July 1903, published 1929.) Its commercial equivalent is the distinction between leading and lagging signal. Conventional market intelligence is backward-looking by design: surveys, data, and analyst reports describe what has already become legible. This work attends to what is visible earlier: in the frustrations of practitioners at the edge, in experiments not yet named as trends. The aim is structured proximity to the frontier: to be present where the next thing is being invented before anyone has named it.

The work is principle-based and non-company-specific. Sources and references are collected at the foot of the page.

01

A Short History of Monetisation

Enterprise software has always been sold as a proxy for the value it creates, but the relationship between price and value has been tightening for thirty years, and the gap is now almost closed.

The earliest commercial software was sold as a capital asset. In the perpetual licence era, the transaction was simple and the logic was borrowed from manufacturing: a buyer paid once for a product, owned it, and the vendor moved on. Price was anchored to the cost of production and the vendor's required margin, with value assumed rather than measured. What it was worth to the buyer was not a question the contract asked.

Maintenance and support contracts arrived not as a concession to customers but as a mechanism for vendors to build recurring revenue on top of one-time sales. The annual maintenance fee, typically ranging from fifteen to twenty-five percent of the original licence, was presented as insurance against the future: access to patches, upgrades, and support. In practice, it was monetised access disguised as insurance.[1]

The SaaS model changed the terms of the relationship in ways that were not fully understood at the time. Subscription pricing made the ongoing nature of the commercial relationship explicit, and renewal became a forcing function. A customer who felt they were not receiving value could, in theory, simply not renew, and this possibility restructured the incentives on both sides of the table. The unit of pricing, however, remained disconnected from outcome: seats, users, and named accounts are measures of access, not of value delivered.

Usage-based pricing was the next step toward alignment. Consumption models that charged by the API call, the transaction, or the compute unit connected price to utilisation, at least. A customer who used the product heavily paid more. But utilisation is not value, and the gap between the two is where most of the commercial tension in enterprise software now lives.

The credit model that proliferated across AI-native commercial deployments during this transition serves a structural function that its name obscures. Credits are an abstraction layer positioned between the customer's agreed price and the vendor's underlying infrastructure cost. When model inference prices shift month to month, a vendor with a credit-denominated rate card can adjust the cost per unit of work without altering the headline price. The customer's bill may change; the price they agreed to pay does not. This mechanism transfers cost volatility from the commercial relationship into the rate card, where it is more tractable for both parties. The design decisions that determine whether this architecture builds the trust it requires include: whether the credit-to-dollar conversion is transparent enough for customers to forecast their own bill, how consumption is tracked and communicated, whether unused credits roll over, and what caps prevent cost surprises at scale. The vendors who resolve these questions before launch can sustain the abstraction as a genuine bridge toward outcome pricing. Those who discover them in production pay a trust cost that is difficult to recover.[33]

The credit mechanism carries a further commercial dimension beyond cost abstraction. Credit quantities function as a lever of offer design, independent of the price itself. Rollovers, initial credit bonuses, free daily allowances, and generous first-period allocations each adjust what the customer receives without changing what they agreed to pay. A buyer with irregular demand can be moved from monthly to annual commitment not through price reduction but through a rollover policy that carries credits into the next period. A buyer hesitant about annual commitment can be won through a doubled first-month credit allocation that represents additional value without a change in list price. A buyer at risk of abandoning the product between intensive work sessions can be retained through a modest free credit allowance that removes the economic friction of re-engagement. What these mechanisms share is that they operate on the value side of the ledger rather than the price side. The list price holds. The offer changes. Credits give vendors a richer surface for commercial negotiation than the seat count or the token rate ever provided, without triggering the margin erosion that list-price discounting produces.[46]

Credits as offer design

Three practitioner cases from the same two-week period illustrate the behavioural dimension of the credit architecture. One video generation platform offers credit rollovers as a default: unused credits carry to the next billing period, specifically addressing the resistance from buyers whose work is project-heavy and uneven. The design converts an objection about paid credits going to waste into a reason to upgrade rather than a reason to decline. A web design platform offers doubled credits in the first month of annual commitment: the offer is a two-month bundle of value at a one-month price, expressed in credits rather than in dollars. The renewal economics remain unchanged; the customer receives more value on day one. A developer coding platform offers free credits at each login: small, sufficient to begin a piece of work, insufficient to complete a meaningful one. The design is precise: the cost of returning to the product after a gap is zero; the cost of staying productive is the subscription. Each case represents the same underlying principle. The credit architecture creates room for offer design that the seat count and the token rate do not. A well-constructed credit offer can move a buyer across a commitment threshold, convert monthly to annual, or reduce the friction of return, without a change to the list price the vendor reports.

A structural refinement emerges from the evidence of AI-native products. The viable pricing unit depends not only on where the market has evolved, but on how broad the product's scope has become. A product designed to do exactly one job can price at the level of that job: the cost per unit is predictable, the value per invocation is estimable, and pricing can sit close to the outcome rather than the mechanism. When an AI product expands from a purpose-built feature into a generalised assistant capable of many jobs, the calculus changes. Each interaction's resource consumption becomes variable in ways that action-level pricing cannot absorb: two exchanges with the same number of prompts may consume dramatically different compute depending on complexity, context, and the internal steps required to complete the work. At that point, pricing must descend toward the underlying resource. The inverse holds with equal force: a vendor who narrows their product's job definition gains the ability to abstract their pricing upward, toward the outcome the customer values, away from the compute that makes it possible. The direction of pricing is set by the direction of product scope.[31]

Scope change, pricing unit change

One workflow automation platform previously charged a flat credit per prompt when its AI feature did exactly one job: generating workflows from natural language. When the feature was expanded into a broader assistant capable of creating, editing, debugging, explaining, and troubleshooting automations, the flat-credit model collapsed. The pricing unit migrated from interactions to tokens. The included credit allocation shifted by two orders of magnitude. What the expanded product could do had increased; what could be predicted about any individual interaction's cost had decreased. The product became more capable and less priceable at the action level simultaneously. This is not a pricing failure. It is the structural consequence of broadening scope. When pricing units change, comparability disappears. Old credits measured interactions; new credits measure inference. It is not possible to determine, from the rate card alone, whether the product became more or less expensive. The loss of comparability is a commercial cost of expanded scope that is rarely named at the moment of the pricing change.

Source: Good Better Best (PricingSaaS), Rob Litterst, "The Broader the Agent, the Finer the Pricing Unit," 24 July 2026[31]

A structural force is now visible that makes the transition from usage to outcome pricing not merely commercially desirable but economically necessary for a growing class of vendor. The cost architecture of AI-native products differs from SaaS in one precise way: inference costs accrue at the moment of work, not at the moment of access. In a classical SaaS deployment, the most active users are typically the most profitable, because the marginal cost of serving them approaches zero. In an AI-native product, the most active users are frequently the most expensive, because each interaction requires a compute cycle whose cost the vendor bears directly. The near-zero marginal cost assumption that underpinned seat-based pricing no longer holds. Pricing must migrate toward the unit of value delivered because the unit of cost has already moved there. The previous transitions were driven by buyer-side pressure; this one is being driven by the vendor's own cost structure.[20]

Where this leads

The logical endpoint of this thirty-year compression is a commercial model in which price is set by reference to the outcome the customer achieves, not the product the vendor delivers. That model exists today in isolated pockets of the enterprise software market.

Each transition in this history has followed a similar pattern. A new pricing model emerges that more accurately reflects the value exchange. Adoption is initially slow: it requires new measurement capabilities and a tolerance for commercial risk on both sides. Then it becomes standard faster than incumbents expect. By most practitioner accounts, the distance between SaaS as a curiosity and SaaS as the default was roughly a decade. There is no particular reason to think the next transition will take longer.

The arc may not stop at usage-based pricing. A further stage is now visible, in which the commercial exchange migrates into the moment of work itself. As AI agents acquire the ability to discover, evaluate, and activate capabilities at the point of need, the separation between procurement decision and deployment collapses. What previously required months of vendor evaluation, proof-of-concept, and internal approval can now be initiated within the environment where the work is being done. The purchasing cycle is not merely shortening. It is being relocated from a governance function into a production environment. If this pattern establishes itself, the next commercial model is not just more closely aligned with value: it is embedded in the delivery of value itself.[5]

02

The Shift, Observed

The clearest evidence that commercial models are changing is not in the pricing pages of software vendors. It is in the budget decisions of their customers, in the duration of their contracts, and in the structural compression of the margins that the previous era took for granted.

Enterprise buyers are arriving at renewal and expansion conversations with substantially clearer data on delivered value than they carried five years ago. FinOps functions, third-party benchmarking services, and internal spend analytics platforms have professionalised the buying process, closing the information gap that protected vendor pricing through most of the SaaS era. A seller who cannot articulate delivered economic value in the customer's own terms faces increasing pressure toward discounting.[2]

The same buyer is also signing for shorter periods. Contract duration, which served for two decades as the most reliable lagging indicator of vendor pricing power, is now compressing faster than the SaaS-era commercial model can adapt to. The behavioural logic is straightforward: in a market where the leading product in a category may be displaced inside a contract term, flexibility is worth more than the discount available for committing to term. Vendor revenue forecasts built on multi-year deals are losing one of their underlying assumptions.[8]

Contract duration in compression

Survey data from a 2026 cross-industry analysis of B2B software companies shows sub-one-year contracts for new logos rising from four percent of deals in 2023 to thirteen percent in 2026. Three-year contracts have fallen from twenty-eight to twenty-three percent over the same period. Sales cycles compressed by roughly a quarter across the second half of 2025, from twenty-five weeks to nineteen. A former chief revenue officer of one of the formative cloud data platforms describes the pattern as a rational response to a market in which leadership positions shift on a quarterly cadence: customers are not hesitant, they are correctly unwilling to lock into a configuration of tools that may be obsolete inside the contract term.

Source: SaaStr, reporting on ICONIQ State of Go-to-Market 2026, April 2026

The automation layer of enterprise software stacks is under particular pressure. Buyers are discovering that purpose-built AI agents can replicate the process automation tools sold as add-ons to core platforms, at a fraction of the cost and time. The implication is not the wholesale replacement of core systems, which carry compliance, integration, and auditability value that point solutions cannot easily replicate. It is the erosion of the module and add-on revenue that made those platforms profitable at scale. Vendors whose margins depend on the automation layer face a more immediate threat than those whose core value lies in data, workflow, or identity.

Signal from the market

A CIO at a data security firm generating over two billion dollars in annual revenue reports that early tests suggest he can cut spending on enterprise software automation tools in half by replacing them with AI agents built in-house. He is keeping the core platforms. He is not renewing the automation add-ons. The incumbents' response, that AI-built alternatives lack the compliance and auditability that regulated enterprises require, is a value argument, not a price one. It may be correct. But it is a harder argument to make at renewal than it was two years ago.

Source: practitioner account, March 2026

A structural repricing is under way in enterprise software, operating below the level of individual product decisions. Foundation model capabilities, accessible directly through API at variable and declining cost, have entered the procurement conversation as a new reference class against which existing tool investments are now being evaluated. The question that an informed buyer brings to renewal is no longer only whether the product justifies the seat price: it is whether the functionality delivered could be approximated, at sufficient quality, using general-purpose AI infrastructure at a fraction of the cost. For vendors whose differentiated value sits in a narrow functional layer built on top of capabilities that are now widely accessible, this is not a negotiation. The new benchmark is not a competing product. It is general-purpose infrastructure.[10]

The new benchmark in a renewal conversation

A practitioner account published in April 2026 documents a real enterprise renewal in which a general-purpose AI integration replicated approximately ninety-five percent of a vendor's differentiated AI feature at fifteen percent of the equivalent token cost. The customer cut the renewal by forty-five percent. What is significant about this account is not the outcome but the mechanism: the ROI benchmark was set not by a competing product but by direct access to general-purpose AI infrastructure. The competitive reference class has expanded beyond the product category itself.

Source: OnlyCFO Newsletter, "Pricing Pressure Will Crush You," April 2026

The vendors furthest into the transition are showing a two-stage pattern that illuminates how the shift from efficiency to value capture unfolds. In stage one, AI is deployed internally to reduce operational costs: support cases handled at scale, staff reassigned, margin protected. In stage two, the same capability is turned outward, toward commercial opportunities that were previously uneconomical to pursue. Prospects too small, too dormant, or too geographically dispersed to justify the cost of human outreach become reachable. Stage two is where the transition from cost model to revenue model occurs.[6]

The two-phase pattern

One enterprise platform provider reports deploying AI agents to handle over three million customer conversations, generating one hundred million dollars in annualised cost savings and reducing case volume by eight percent. In the second phase, the same infrastructure was redirected toward dormant commercial opportunities previously considered uneconomical to pursue. The result was more than 3,200 influenced sales opportunities. The language used internally for this second phase was precise: turning "sawdust" into pipeline. Sawdust is the byproduct of work, previously valueless, now a feedstock for something new. The commercial logic of staying in stage one, once stage two has been demonstrated, is difficult to sustain.

Source: Fortune, April 2026
Two bets on the same transition

In 2024, two well-funded legal AI companies served comparable enterprise clients from similar competitive positions. One built infrastructure: GPU capacity, model fine-tuning, proprietary reasoning systems, and data flywheels that compounded with each inference. Every dollar went into compute. The other built a managed service: human reviewers assisted by AI tools, a delivery model whose costs scaled linearly with revenue. By late 2025, the infrastructure-first company had reached $190 million in annualised revenue at an $11 billion valuation. The managed-services company failed to close a funding round and entered a distressed sale. The divergence was not primarily about which product customers preferred. It was about which cost structure could compound. One had better unit economics per customer. The other had compounding physics. By the time the difference registered in the data, the gap was irreversible.

The cost of acquisition-led growth: aggregate evidence

Analysis of more than 72 public SaaS companies shows that customer acquisition cost roughly doubled and annual growth rates roughly halved between 2021 and 2026. Parallel deterioration is reported across major investor portfolios. The structural diagnosis emerging from practitioner research is that two decades of acquisition-led commercial models, built around generating pipeline from outside the customer base, have reached a ceiling. Growth loops, which generate the next wave of pipeline from inside an existing customer base through retention, expansion, and advocacy, do something economically different: unlike a funnel, whose costs scale with its output, a loop grows the system's capacity to generate pipeline with each customer who achieves measurable value. The connection to outcome pricing is direct. A loop cannot run on disconnected signals or unverified outcomes. It requires exactly the measurement infrastructure that outcome-based commercial models are designed to produce.

Source: Jacco van der Kooij, "How AI Turns Fragmented GTM Workflows Into Coordinated Growth Systems," Winning by Design Growth Institute, June 5, 2026[26]

Adoption data from the lower end of the enterprise market is showing a pattern that has preceded disruption in adjacent software categories. AI-native challengers with purpose-built commercial models, including pricing structures linked to customer outcomes and significantly lower total cost of entry, are taking measurable market share from incumbents among companies below one hundred million dollars in revenue. This is the cohort with the lowest switching costs and the least institutional inertia in procurement. It is typically where new commercial models prove themselves before moving upmarket.[3]

A second structural force is now visible on the supply side of the market. The unit economics of enterprise software are changing as AI inference, model routing, and vector database infrastructure enter the cost of goods sold. The classic SaaS margin profile, built on near-zero marginal cost of delivery, is being compressed for any product with meaningful AI content. Practitioner surveys are placing expected gross margins for AI-native products in the low fifties, against the seventy to eighty percent that defined the previous era. The commercial response is not to defend the old margin profile, which the underlying cost structure no longer supports. It is to redesign the pricing model around the layer where value and cost both accrue: a platform fee that prices access, combined with token or output-linked components that recover variable cost in proportion to consumption. Hybrid pricing of this form is now the primary commercial model for nearly half of B2B companies with embedded AI. The shift from transactional to value-based pricing is no longer only a buyer-led story. It is being forced by the economics on the vendor side as well.[9]

The margin floor is moving

A practitioner analysis of B2B software companies with embedded AI puts expected average gross margins at fifty-two percent for 2026, against the seventy-to-eighty percent that defined the SaaS era. The cause is structural: AI inference, model routing, and vector infrastructure are direct variable costs incurred at the moment of work, behaving more like a utility input than like the near-zero marginal cost of classical hosting. The pricing response taking shape is hybrid in form, combining a platform fee that prices access with token or output-linked components that price use. This is the commercial geometry of a model in which both cost and value occur in the act of consumption, and the contract has to recognise both. A founder building in the category puts the design principle directly: the platform layer prices the relationship; the token layer prices the work.

Source: practitioner analyses, ICONIQ 2026 State of AI, and founder commentary, April 2026
When token prices fall and bills explode

Token prices fell from sixty dollars per million to sixty cents over three years: a hundred-fold reduction. In the same period, AI bills expanded. A four-person startup incurred a $113,000 monthly bill from a single model provider. A technology company with large engineering teams burned through its entire annual AI budget within four months. The cause is structural rather than accidental. An AI agent does not consume tokens the way a single query does: it re-reads context, checks its own work, and generates intermediate steps, consuming sixty to one hundred and forty times the tokens of a direct reply. Token pricing, like seat pricing before it, is a proxy for value that breaks down at the scale of agent-driven work. The practitioner conclusion already emerging is direct: track cost per completed task, not per seat and not per token. The unit of account must become the unit of value delivered.

Source: "Are AI Employees More Expensive Than Humans?" lex.substack.com, reported in TLDR Founders, 3 July 2026[27]

The token economics of enterprise AI in mid-2026 are producing a dynamic that runs in both directions simultaneously. At the level of the individual AI agent, token consumption can multiply to sixty or one hundred and forty times that of a direct query. Bills expand regardless of the headline price per million tokens. At the level of aggregate market spend across companies with more typical AI workloads, price deflation is now moving faster than consumption growth: the average cost per million tokens fell by more than forty percent between March and August 2026, causing bills to shrink even at the heaviest-spending companies. Vendor revenue for AI products is consequently facing structural compression from both sides: the agent-intensive customers running the highest-value workloads are generating costs that token pricing cannot sustainably absorb, while the typical customer sees their bill fall without reducing usage. Neither dynamic is temporary. Both are structural features of a market in which the underlying infrastructure is commoditising faster than the commercial models are adapting.[27]

When token prices fall and bills shrink: the other side of the ledger

Card data from a corporate spend platform covering over seventy thousand companies finds that AI spend per employee fell approximately ten percent in August 2026 at the companies spending most heavily on AI, reaching an average of seven thousand two hundred dollars per employee. The cause is structural: the average cost per million tokens dropped from a March 2026 peak of $1.15 to $0.68 by August, a decline of more than forty percent. The operational implication is direct: customers are consuming the same volume of AI capability and paying less for it, without reducing their usage. Vendors are earning less per unit of work performed even as the market grows. The observation that follows is structural: falling token prices transfer the benefit of model efficiency improvements to the buyer rather than to the vendor. At scale, this dynamic intensifies the pressure to move pricing away from the consumption unit toward a metric more closely tied to the outcome the customer values rather than the compute the vendor expends. If the outcome remains constant while the underlying token cost continues to fall, the outcome price is substantially more defensible than the token price. The direction of pricing pressure and the direction of the transition argument point the same way.

Source: TechCrunch (2026). AI spend per employee slumped at top firms in August. September 9, 2026. Citing Ramp corporate card data, 70,000 companies.[42]

The commercial response to this new cost structure is not uniform, and the most instructive divergence is between vendors who have moved outcome pricing from commercial thesis to live contract and those who have not. The architecture emerging from the earliest AI-native deployments suggests a three-layer approach that addresses the specific failure modes of the classical SaaS freemium model in an AI context. The first layer gates intensity rather than capability, charging for volume, context length, and compute priority rather than for access to features. The second layer gates outcomes rather than access: the customer pays for confirmed value delivered, not for the opportunity to attempt delivery. The third layer gates the heaviest compute modalities, whose cost of delivery at scale is prohibitive regardless of pricing tier. What distinguishes this architecture from its predecessors is not the mechanics but the principle: the paywall sits on the unit of work the buyer cares about, not on the feature that produces it.[15]

The outcome layer in practice

One AI-native customer service platform is currently priced at $0.99 per resolved support ticket. The service is free to attempt; payment is triggered only when the customer confirms the problem was actually fixed. This is outcome-based pricing in live commercial deployment, operating at scale, not in pilot or strategic account structure. Practitioners working on AI pricing are naming the structural reason this approach matters: the classical SaaS freemium model creates what they are calling an AI freemium paradox. If the free tier is powerful enough to generate genuine use, users question why they should pay. If it is too restricted to demonstrate the product's capability, conversion never occurs. The outcome layer bypasses both: the customer pays for what was useful, not for access to what might be useful. The distinction is not a pricing tweak. It is a different theory of what is being sold.

Source: Arnon Shimoni, "Pricing is a Product Surface," arnon.dk, 5 May 2026; citing practitioner analysis of Google AI subscriptions and Intercom Fin commercial structure

The structural failure of classical freemium in AI-native products is not only an acquisition problem. It is a cost problem. Classic freemium assumed zero marginal cost of free: giving away a template, a dashboard slot, or a search query cost the vendor nothing at the infrastructure level. AI inference does not share this property. Every free generation consumed against a language model carries a real compute cost that the vendor bears directly. The calculus of free value has changed at its foundation. A vendor offering free AI capabilities is subsidising the customer's use of inference infrastructure on the assumption that the habit formed will convert. When the habit fails to convert, or when the customer's routine work can be handled adequately by a general-purpose AI tool at no cost to them, the subsidy produces neither revenue nor retention. A further mechanism compounds this: for much of the work that free tiers previously did (the first draft, the quick design mockup, the one-off script, the rough analysis), a general-purpose language model is now the effective free tier, accessible without subscription. The vendor whose freemium tier once competed on access no longer wins on cost alone. The response emerging from the most sophisticated AI-native commercial teams is a structural reorientation of what free is for: from acquisition to retention, from converting non-customers to protecting the credit balance of paying ones.[43]

Free value, restructured

A cross-market survey tracking more than 4,300 software companies finds that 36 percent still offer a free tier, and that companies with credit-based pricing are nearly twice as likely to offer free plans as those without (65 percent against 36 percent). The pattern emerging from the most active credit-model operators is not the removal of free but its relocation across four observable mechanisms. Free by model routes the cheaper model at no credit cost while directing intensive work to the frontier tier that draws the wallet. Free by speed offers the same model on a slower queue, preserving the credit balance for priority work. Free by action type excludes specific interaction modes from credit consumption entirely: chat, voice dictation, or document search, at the vendor's cost, in exchange for adoption depth. Free refill adds a daily or weekly credit allocation on top of the purchased balance, protecting the wallet from routine work so the remainder is available for the high-value work the vendor wants to price. What these four patterns share is that free value, in a credit-denominated commercial model, is not a design choice about whether to offer it. It is a design choice about what the customer's balance is reserved for. The structural consequence is direct: free-inside-paid is a retention mechanism, not an acquisition one. The customer whose habit it sustains is already paying.

Source: Litterst, R. (2026). "Free Mode and the Future of Freemium." Good Better Best (PricingSaaS newsletter), 18 September 2026[43]

What distinguishes the present moment from earlier phases of this transition is the movement of the outcome model from AI-native challengers into the incumbent cohort. When a major incumbent in a category publicly retires its deflection-based pricing and restructures its commercial model around verified resolution, it does two things at once: it confirms that the architecture is viable at enterprise scale, not only in the conditions favourable to a challenger, and it shifts the competitive pressure onto others in the category who have not yet moved. A model that was once an experiment at the edge is becoming a commercial baseline that others are expected to explain their distance from.[19]

Incumbents commit to outcome billing

At its annual customer conference in May 2026, one of the largest enterprise customer service platforms announced that its AI agents would be priced solely on verifiably resolved outcomes, retiring the deflection-based pricing that has defined the category for two decades. Verification is two-stage: the resolving AI agent and an independent AI evaluation model both confirm the resolution; routine exchanges are excluded. The platform underpinning the commitment has been trained on roughly twenty billion customer service interactions. The company's chief executive stated publicly that AI agents would handle more customer service interactions than humans during the current year, with a five-year projection in which the majority of such interactions would be AI-to-AI. This is not a pilot or a commercial experiment at the margin. It is a business-model commitment, at scale, by a major incumbent. When AI-native challengers first adopted outcome-based pricing, the argument against generalising the pattern was that the model was too complex to operate at enterprise scale. That argument is now harder to make.

Source: Zendesk, Relate 2026 conference and newsroom announcement, May 19, 2026

The transition from practitioner experiment to enterprise norm is now measurable in adoption data for the first time. A cross-industry survey of enterprise software decision makers conducted in the first half of 2026 finds that eighteen point seven percent of enterprises now use outcome-based pricing tied to agreed-upon metrics. This approaches parity, for the first time, with per-user, per-month models at seventeen point one percent. More than half of buyers identify pricing model as a key purchase decision criterion. This is the first empirical indicator that outcome-based pricing has crossed from pioneer adoption into competitive standard: a rate that approaches parity with the incumbent model is not an edge case that requires both parties to take an unusual commercial risk. It is a market segment large enough to anchor a procurement conversation.[25]

Pay-per-resolution at scale: the largest incumbent commits

In June 2026, the largest vendor in the enterprise customer relationship management category launched a customer service agent priced at two dollars per fully resolved interaction, deployed initially on its own help portal, where the model achieved a seventy percent resolution rate across four point three million inquiries. Payment is triggered only when the issue is resolved autonomously from start to finish; interactions where the customer requests a human transfer, or leaves without resolution, are not charged. The vendor describes the design principle as "pay only for success." Two structural observations follow. First, the vendor has tested the model against its own demand before commercialising it: the verification mechanism carries the vendor's own operational credibility before it reaches the buyer's procurement table. Second, the entry of the largest incumbent in the category to pay-per-resolution at this price point changes the competitive reference class for every other vendor in the resolution space. Smaller and mid-market platforms that have operated outcome-based models since 2022 will now be positioned, from the buyer's perspective, as precursors to an enterprise-scale model rather than as innovators in a niche one. The analyst commentary accompanying the announcement identifies governance, not capability, as the gating factor for scale: the question of whether enterprises will trust the verification mechanism is the primary constraint on how fast the model spreads.

Source: Salesforce newsroom, Agentforce Help Agent announcement, June 25, 2026; Futurum Group, Kirkpatrick, K., "Salesforce's Agentforce Help Agent Bets on Pay-Per-Resolution: Will Enterprises Trust the Model?" June 25, 2026[25]
Outcome ARR at scale: Q2 FY2027

In the second quarter of fiscal year 2027, the largest vendor in the enterprise customer relationship management category reported annual recurring revenue for its AI agent product exceeding one and a half billion dollars, representing growth of more than two hundred and forty percent year on year. The same company announced the expansion of its AI offer into three commercial tiers structured around capability access and consumption commitment, alongside the completion of an acquisition to extend its outcome-billing footprint into customer service. What the data confirms is the trajectory: a pay-per-resolution commercial model launched in June 2026 against the company's own support demand has, within a single reporting cycle, advanced from operational proof to confirmed revenue line at scale. The distance between pilot credibility and scale credibility in outcome-based pricing is shorter than most analyst timelines implied twelve months ago.

A further dimension of this transition is not yet visible in aggregate market data, and for structural rather than statistical reasons. Under annual contracts, the gap between when a buyer decides not to renew and when that decision registers in renewal data is the length of the remaining contract term. Practitioner accounts from April 2026 suggest that ninety percent or more of AI-driven churn is absent from market statistics for a structural reason: the decisions to leave have already been made, but the contracts under which those buyers are held have not yet expired. The buyers who will not renew have already stopped investing attention in those products, redirected budget to AI infrastructure, and begun the parallel experiments that will replace them. The churn signal is present; it is running approximately one contract cycle ahead of the data.[12]

The year of churn: mechanisms

A practitioner CFO writing in April 2026 describes ten specific mechanisms by which software contracts are being cut in 2026. The structural observation that sits above them is this: ninety percent or more of AI-driven churn has not yet appeared in renewal data because the relevant contracts have not yet expired. The decisions are made; they are not yet visible. The trust architecture of usage-based pricing has emerged as a distinct commercial problem, separate from the design of the pricing structure itself. The View from Three Years Out

The future of enterprise value is not waiting to be modelled. It is waiting to be perceived. The enterprises that will define commercial norms in three years are already running experiments the rest of the market regards as too complex to scale. They are not too complex. They are too unfamiliar.

A historical precedent makes the direction of this transition legible even before its destination is visible. When a more efficient steam engine made coal-powered energy dramatically cheaper in the late eighteenth century, the intuitive expectation was that demand for coal would fall. What followed was the reverse: consumption expanded across the following century. Cheaper energy had not reduced the use of energy; it had expanded the range of processes it became economical to attempt.

The same dynamic is now visible in AI-assisted knowledge work. As the cost of finding, synthesising, and translating information collapses, the practical consequence is not a reduction in the demand for knowledge work but a redistribution of where value sits within it. When synthesis and retrieval approach near-zero cost, the constraint migrates to what remains scarce: judgment, interpretation, and the capacity to produce something genuinely new from cheap material.

Vendors whose value proposition rests on access to information, or on executing processes that information makes possible, are being repriced by the same structural dynamic that repriced energy two centuries ago. The next pricing power sits in the layer where human judgment remains irreducible and cannot be automated at the same falling cost.[18]

Outcome-based pricing, currently confined to large strategic accounts with high data maturity on both sides, will become a standard option in enterprise software negotiations within three years. Shared data environments and agreed measurement frameworks are maturing faster than the commercial frameworks for using them. The constraint is not technical. It is the absence of commercial practitioners who know how to structure, negotiate, and manage outcome-linked contracts at scale.

The unbundling of enterprise platforms will continue, but not uniformly. Vendors with strong claims on identity, workflow, and compliance infrastructure will hold their core positions. The economics of replacing a system of record that sits at the centre of a company's operational data flows are prohibitive for most buyers regardless of the available alternatives. What will not hold is the pricing power that platform vendors have historically extracted from the layers built on top of those cores: the automation tools, the analytics modules, the integration middleware. Those are the positions that AI-native alternatives are targeting, and they will succeed in the accounts where the compliance argument is weakest.

The unbundling dynamic has a further dimension not yet widely visible in commercial strategy. As AI agents acquire the ability to access enterprise platforms programmatically, the interface layer that historically bundled value with usage begins to separate. A buyer may maintain access to the full data and workflow infrastructure of an enterprise platform while never directly interacting with the vendor's interface. The platform becomes headless in practice, accessed entirely through an agent that routes requests, retrieves data, and executes actions invisibly to the user. Where this pattern establishes itself, the vendor retains the data relationship but loses the daily interaction that sustains the perceived value of the product, the upsell opportunity that the interface creates, and the user habit that makes the next renewal straightforward. The commercial logic of this separation has not yet been priced into most enterprise software valuations.[13]

The emerging discipline

Value engineering, currently a support function within enterprise software sales organisations, is becoming a core commercial discipline with its own data systems, methodologies, and board visibility. Companies that invest in this capability before their competitors will price more effectively, retain customers more reliably, and expand revenue with less friction. The gap between those who do and those who do not will be visible in net revenue retention figures within two to three years.

The enterprises building toward this frontier are already visible, not in the data that analysts are collecting but in the places analysts are not yet looking: research environments, early developer communities, and the work of practitioners attempting the most demanding version of the value measurement and pricing problem. The firms closest to where the next generation of AI infrastructure is being built will be able to see where the market is heading before it registers in aggregate data. The others will discover the shift when it appears in renewal data.

A structural signal arrived in June 2026 that extends the transition further than this analysis has previously traced. The infrastructure layer for commercial exchange itself is being built to accommodate non-human actors. In the same week, major payment network operators announced dedicated architectures enabling AI agents to initiate, authorise, and complete financial transactions without requiring human instruction at the moment of purchase. The commercial implication is not primarily about payment technology. It is about who the commercial counterparty is anticipated to be. Investment decisions at the infrastructure level of payment networks are made against long planning horizons. The thesis embedded in these announcements is that AI agents will become independent commercial actors at sufficient scale, within the timeframe relevant to infrastructure investment, to justify dedicated network architecture. If that thesis proves correct, the transition described in this publication does not end with outcome-based pricing for human buyers. It extends to a commercial environment in which the buyer itself may be a machine.[22]

The payment rails being built for agents

In a single week in June 2026, two of the largest global payment network operators announced commercial infrastructure for AI-agent-initiated transactions: one, a card platform enabling AI agents to make purchases, verify credentials, and transact autonomously; the other, a partnership with a major AI systems developer enabling models to complete purchases and bookings without human intervention. Both organisations are building for a commercial environment in which AI agents are not assistants to the purchasing decision but participants in it. Payment infrastructure investments at this scale and with this degree of coordination do not describe an anticipated edge case. They describe an anticipated norm. The question this raises for commercial model design is direct: what does a revenue architecture look like when the buyer is an agent running on behalf of an agent, with no human in the decision loop?

Source: Reuters and financial press, reporting on Mastercard Agent Pay for Machines and Visa agentic commerce infrastructure partnership, June 2026

The role of the buyer will also change in ways that most sellers have not yet modelled. As AI-assisted procurement becomes standard, buyers will arrive at commercial conversations with more precise value benchmarks, better alternatives analysis, and a clearer view of their own usage patterns than most vendors currently have. Sellers have historically known what a product is worth to a customer better than the customer does. That advantage is narrowing. Vendors who have built their sales approach around that advantage have less time to adapt than they may assume.

The provocation

The professional instinct in commercial strategy is to resolve, clarify, model, present: to have an answer. The artists and poets who have most clearly perceived what analysts missed did something different: they waited. They held unresolved questions without urgency to close them, and in that waiting, something became visible that urgency prevents. The question is not what the future of enterprise value will look like. It is: what would it mean to gestate a pricing model rather than build one? To live inside the question of value long enough that the answer surfaces?

The Case Against

The transition narrative underestimates the incumbents' structural advantages: the cost of compliance, the depth of integration, the institutional logic of enterprise procurement, and the practical constraint that forces some buyers away from variable-cost models regardless of their alignment with value.

Compliance and auditability are not features sitting alongside the product. For regulated industries, they are the product. For regulated buyers, the ability to show an auditor, regulator, or board exactly how a business process was executed is worth more than any efficiency gain an alternative can offer: which step was authorised by whom, and what the system state was at any point, must be demonstrable on demand. AI-built replacements do not yet carry the compliance and auditability assurance that enterprise procurement in regulated industries requires. Capability is not in question; auditability is. Incumbent platforms in regulated sectors hold positions the transition narrative tends to understate.[4]

Switching costs in mature enterprise software relationships are structural rather than psychological. The integration network of a mature platform represents an investment that is difficult to replicate and costly to abandon: the data flows it manages, the API dependencies built on top of it, the trained user base, and the customisations and configurations accumulated over years. The financial case for replacing a well-embedded system has to be overwhelming before a procurement function will recommend it, and the bar rises with the size and complexity of the organisation. AI has lowered the cost of routing around a vendor whose product is failing at the margin. Where a specific function is broken or a vendor's rate of change has stalled, a technically literate operator can now replicate or substitute that function faster than the vendor can fix it. The structural lock-in of a well-embedded core system remains real; the protection it extends to individual features and peripheral layers within that system is narrowing.

Two incumbent responses to the headless transition

The transition to headless and agent-mediated access is producing structurally different responses within the incumbent cohort, and both are deliberate. One pattern, already documented in this publication, is proactive: build the headless layer, govern its absence, and reposition the platform as the substrate of work rather than its surface. A second pattern moves in the opposite direction: restrict programmatic access by external AI agents and require customers to route all AI interactions through a proprietary layer. The commercial rationale for the closed strategy is clear. If the proprietary AI interface is the only authorised route to a platform's data, the data position is monetised twice. The early evidence on this bet is cautionary: reported adoption of the proprietary AI layer in at least one major platform pursuing this strategy stands at approximately three percent of the customer base. A lock-in mechanism that has not been adopted is not a lock. The strategy may still succeed as capabilities develop. But where it fails, it will have generated friction and customer resistance without the retention it was designed to produce.

Source: TLDR IT, reporting on divergent incumbent API strategies, 15 May 2026; TechZine, "ServiceNow goes headless and opens its platform," 14 May 2026

A further constraint on the transition is organisational rather than commercial in origin. Usage-based pricing aligns cost with consumption, but it also makes cost unpredictable in a way that procurement functions are designed to resist. In environments where AI inference costs are variable and potentially unbounded, buyers with large development or operational teams face the risk that a commercial model chosen as a proxy for value becomes an open-ended cost commitment. The organisational response is not to embrace value-aligned pricing but to retreat to flat-rate predictability. The forces pushing vendors toward consumption pricing are simultaneously pushing some buyers back toward subscription. This is not a transitional friction that resolves as the market matures. It is a structural tension between the vendor's need to recover variable AI infrastructure cost and the buyer's need to control budget. Understanding the model does not resolve it.[24]

Buyer retreat: when variable cost becomes uncontrollable

In June 2026, two pricing decisions in the AI coding tools market illustrated the structural tension from opposite sides of the table. A major technology company withdrew its software engineering teams from a usage-based AI coding tool, citing per-engineer token costs of approximately $2,000 per month, and reassigned them to a flat-rate alternative. In the same period, the platform managing the flat-rate alternative converted every plan to usage-based AI-credit billing. The vendor is moving toward consumption pricing to recover variable AI infrastructure cost; the buyer is moving away from it to protect budget predictability. Both decisions are rational responses to the same underlying dynamic: in an AI-native product, the marginal cost of intensive use is real. The pricing model can shift who holds the variable cost exposure, but it cannot eliminate the exposure. Buyers who discover this at scale, as AI usage intensifies inside their organisations, will negotiate toward the model that protects budget control, regardless of its alignment with value delivered. The transition to value-based pricing does not face only institutional resistance. It faces a genuine cost arithmetic problem for buyers whose usage patterns generate costs that flat-rate pricing was designed to make invisible.

Source: TLDR Founders, "AI Coding Tools Are Quietly Re-Pricing Themselves," reporting on seat economics in AI developer tooling, June 2026[24]

The buying process in large enterprises is designed around a goal that is not value optimisation. It is risk elimination. The vendor that wins a large account is often not the one with the best product or the most favourable commercial model. It is the one that presents the smallest institutional risk to the person signing the contract. Until the risk profile of newer commercial models and newer vendors is well-established across multiple comparable deployments, the incumbents' position in large enterprise is protected by something more durable than product quality or pricing. It is protected by the structure of the buying decision itself.

A further dimension of this institutional inertia is visible at the level of governance itself. The people who sit on the boards of large enterprises were shaped by different market conditions and have developed different intuitions about which risks matter most. One set of reflexes prioritises growth above all; another prioritises survival and cash preservation; a third attends to the risk of becoming structurally irrelevant to a market whose requirements have shifted. Each of these reflexes was rational in the era that formed it. The difficulty is that all three remain defensible in the present tense: growth initiatives still produce pipeline, cost discipline still extends runway, and the risk of irrelevance has not yet registered legibly in the conventional metrics boards use to govern. The dominant risk in the current environment is not insolvency or lost growth. It is the more elusive and slower-signalling risk that a business becomes irrelevant before the data confirms it. Traditional metrics (growth rate, free cash flow margin, and net revenue retention) remain useful and will continue to produce actionable signals in the short term. They do not, however, resolve the question of where the business will stand when those signals arrive. Boards that wait for irrelevance to register in conventional metrics may be running a governance cycle behind the market they are trying to govern.[34]

The dominant risk has shifted

A structural analysis of three eras of enterprise software commercial dynamics, drawing on benchmarking data from more than seventy public SaaS companies, identifies a shift in boardroom risk that maps directly onto the pace of commercial model transition. In the era of abundant capital through 2022, the dominant risk was missing growth: inflated acquisition cost was survivable as long as capital remained accessible. In the correction era from 2022 to 2024, the dominant risk was insolvency: growth could wait, but the balance sheet could not be endangered. The current era is defined by capital concentration rather than capital abundance or capital scarcity: more than sixty percent of venture investment now flows to AI-oriented companies, a degree of concentration exceeding any prior technology wave. In this environment, the dominant risk is irrelevance, and it arrives in the data later, and more quietly, than either of the prior risks did. A business can continue to grow, protect its margins, and buy time while the market has already moved on from it. The signals of impending irrelevance surface outside the metrics boards have relied on for years: in where capital is concentrating, in how quickly a new class of alternatives is advancing, and in what customers are quietly building or evaluating as replacements. By the time those signals reach conventional metrics, the window to respond may have closed.

Source: David Spitz and Jacco van der Kooij, "Sellers, Savers, and Innovators: How Three Board Reflexes Collide in the AI Era," Winning by Design Growth Journal, January 2026[34]

A fourth constraint is organisational capability rather than institutional structure. Survey analysis based on more than 3,800 CIO and technology executive interactions globally finds that only seventeen percent of enterprises are operating at the level of AI maturity required to execute and scale high-value AI use cases. High-maturity organisations deploy AI initiatives at twice the rate of medium-maturity organisations and more than five times the rate of low-maturity ones. The gap is not primarily technological. It is the absence of governance structures, decision-making processes, and AI literacy needed to translate experimentation into operational value at scale.[7]

This observation, from a CIO in financial services, reflects a pattern appearing consistently across industries. The transition to value-based commercial models requires buyers capable of measuring, governing, and demonstrating value with enough precision to anchor an outcome-linked contract. Where the organisational infrastructure for that does not yet exist, the transition stalls regardless of what is available in the market. The 83% of enterprises not yet at high AI maturity represent both the eventual scale of the opportunity and the structural reason it will take longer to arrive.

The adoption-to-value gap in a single sector

In the customer experience sector, AI adoption has reached seventy percent of organisations, up from fifty-seven percent the previous year. Of those who have adopted AI in CX, two percent are seeing genuine value in the form of improved outcomes and measured ROI. Three-quarters of enterprises that deployed a customer-facing AI agent subsequently rolled it back; governance failure was the leading cause, followed by customer data exposure and hallucination or brand risk. The scale of deployment and the rarity of measurable value are not contradictory data points. They describe the same gap that aggregate AI maturity analysis identifies, now visible in a specific commercial context where the outcome metric is among the most precisely defined in enterprise software. High deployment rates and low value realisation rates can coexist because deployment is measured at the moment of launch, and value is measured against what comes after. The seventy-percent figure describes a lagging state; the two-percent figure describes a leading one.

What this means for the thesis

The transition to value-based commercial models will not be uniform across the market. It will be fastest in the segments with the lowest compliance burden, the shortest deployment history, and the most sophisticated buyers. It will be slowest in exactly the accounts that matter most to the incumbents' revenue base. Both things can be true simultaneously: the model is changing, and the incumbents have more time than the disruption narrative implies.

A fifth counterargument sits in the market data itself. Analysis of enterprise software spending patterns as of early 2026 finds that the vast majority of spend remains anchored in seat-based models, and that consumption-based revenue has barely moved, despite the consistent expectation among market commentators that the shift was imminent. The transition narrative is currently running ahead of buyer behaviour. The gap is not evidence that the direction is wrong. It is a measure of the distance between the moment of perception and the moment of practice: between the leading practitioners who have demonstrated the new model and the institutional majority who have not yet assembled the conditions to replicate it.[11]

Analyst evidence: the consumption ramp is slower than anticipated

On 9 July 2026, a major US investment bank downgraded one of the largest enterprise AI platform vendors from its highest conviction recommendation, citing limited proof that its AI agent product was driving near-term revenue growth and warning that consumption-based revenue would take longer to ramp than market expectations implied. The specific concern was data readiness: the vendor's customers were struggling to prepare their data infrastructure for meaningful AI work, and the product itself remained immature for the most demanding enterprise use cases. The vendor disputes the assessment, pointing to an annual recurring revenue figure for its AI agent product that has grown more than two hundred percent year on year. Both accounts can be simultaneously correct. Rapid ARR growth and slower-than-anticipated consumption-based revenue are not contradictory in a market where initial contracts are structured as access commitments rather than consumption commitments. The consumption signal, like the churn signal documented in this section, may be running ahead of the data by approximately one contract cycle.[30]

The partner ecosystem signal, two years in

Two years after the launch of the largest enterprise CRM platform's AI agent product, a survey of implementation partners across the US, Europe, and Asia finds that none are seeing the product drive bookings activity. Its implementation ecosystem reports zero bookings conversion. Both data points can be simultaneously accurate. The ARR figure measures access commitments; the partner figure measures conversion from access to active commercial deployment. The commercial transition also carries a constraint that operates below the level of organisational AI maturity and above the level of billing infrastructure: the absence of commercial governance mechanisms capable of sustaining the execution discipline that value-based models require. Practitioner analysis of recurring revenue businesses across a range of scales finds that the transition fails not primarily from strategic error but from a predictable cascade that originates in incentive design and propagates through the organisation. When the commercial incentive system is optimised for short-term bookings rather than long-term customer value, qualification decisions soften under pipeline pressure; softened qualification expands the customer base to include accounts whose cost to serve exceeds their expected value; discounting rises to close deals that a tighter qualification framework would have declined; and the resulting cohort produces net revenue retention outcomes that amplify the original pressure rather than resolving it. Three reinforcing loops sustain this cascade once it begins: misaligned incentives produce misaligned qualification; misaligned qualification produces customer cohorts that underperform on retention; underperforming retention produces the commercial pressure that makes incentive correction most difficult precisely when it is most necessary. The transition to outcome-based commercial models requires not only a different pricing mechanism but a different governance architecture to sustain the execution discipline that mechanism demands. Where that architecture does not exist, the transition stalls at the deal level regardless of what the pricing page states or what the revenue strategy prescribes.[44]

Governance failure at the deal level: five cases

The evidence from practitioner cases across a range of scale is precise and reproducible. A business at $120 million ARR took an account outside its ideal customer profile at a twenty-eight percent discount; that account renewed at fifty percent of its original contract value. A business at $300 million ARR began the year with fourteen compensation exceptions and closed it with forty-three, effectively operating a shadow compensation plan that displaced the incentive architecture it was nominally running. A business at $250 million ARR allowed three regional teams to apply different qualification criteria; the resulting commercial incoherence produced six points of gross margin compression, representing fifteen million dollars of annualised loss attributable to misaligned qualification decisions rather than market conditions. A fourth business, at $400 million ARR, introduced formal disqualification discipline and increased its win rate from thirty-one to forty-four percent while improving cohort net revenue retention from one hundred and eight to one hundred and twenty-one percent. A fifth, an enterprise at $300 million ARR that maintained qualification discipline through the 2022 to 2023 market correction, produced a net revenue retention of one hundred and thirty-four percent against an industry median of one hundred and nine. The observation is structural rather than prescriptive: the governance mechanisms that produce these outcomes are not difficult to specify. They are difficult to sustain under the commercial pressure that makes them most necessary, and that pressure is highest in exactly the businesses attempting the transition to value-based models.

A concurrent signal from the implementation partner layer extends the observation. As major enterprise platforms move simultaneously toward bundled consumption, pure consumption, and outcomes-based pricing within a single commercial cycle, the implementation partners who translate those architectures to enterprise clients face a practical consequence: they cannot build predictable cost models for buyers when the vendor's own charge-out rates remain unspecified. A senior representative of a major global consultancy has named the constraint publicly: uncertainty in the AI agent capacity rate card makes it structurally impossible to propose a business case with the confidence that initiates a buying process. The vendor's own acknowledgement that pricing calculators and rate sheets are forthcoming but not yet available confirms the constraint is real rather than perceived. Implementation partners matter for this analysis because they are the commercial channel through which most large enterprise AI deployments are proposed, structured, and initiated. When that channel cannot model the economics of a deployment, the pipeline that would confirm or challenge the commercial model does not begin.[45]

A sixth constraint on pricing velocity operates on the vendor side and receives less attention in the transition narrative than the buyer-side pressures documented above. The billing systems on which most enterprise software vendors operate were designed for the commercial model of the previous era. Subscription billing infrastructure built for flat-rate seat access was not designed to meter token consumption, verify outcome events, or aggregate resolution counts at the granularity that usage- and outcome-based models require. The constraint is not cosmetic. Vendors who want to migrate toward consumption or outcome pricing first have to change what their systems can represent, which is typically a months-long infrastructure project that costs considerably more than the pricing design itself. More consequentially, existing billing infrastructure is a repository of every commercial commitment made in prior periods: the custom rates, the special entitlements, the arrangements that were once a sales tactic and are now a structural constraint. Each must be renegotiated or grandfathered before a new pricing model can be applied uniformly.

The accumulated total of bespoke commercial commitments constitutes what practitioners working on pricing velocity are beginning to name as commercial debt. Commercial debt accumulates each time a sales representative resolves a deal outside the standard model: a custom rate agreed verbally rather than documented in a contract, a carved-out entitlement whose operational cost sits invisibly across the organisation. As that variance compounds across an installed base, the ability to make a uniform pricing move declines, because fewer and fewer customers share the same commercial terms. The billing system cannot be changed until the contracts can be changed. The contracts cannot be changed uniformly until the debt is addressed. And the debt is invisible on any balance sheet, which is precisely why it is rarely paid down.[36]

Pricing velocity and the infrastructure gap

The speed at which AI-native companies experiment with pricing, multiple material changes within a single month, core plan terms reset within days of model provider price adjustments, is not primarily evidence of superior commercial conviction. It is evidence of a different commercial infrastructure. Companies building their billing and contract systems from scratch carry no legacy commitments and no accumulated variance in their contract book. Those operating on commercial infrastructure built a decade or more ago inherit every promise that infrastructure was designed to honour. The gap between the two is not closeable through better commercial strategy alone; it is an infrastructure remediation project, carried out while the business continues to operate and while competitors with cleaner systems make more experiments and learn faster. The combined effect of the billing constraint and commercial debt is that pricing experimentation in a market that rewards rapid learning compounds against incumbents in the same way that any capability gap compounds when the market is accelerating. A practical test from practitioners working on the problem: if it would take a quarter to run a pricing experiment that should take a month, the constraint is almost certainly in the billing system or the contract book, not in the strategy.

The transition narrative overstates incumbents' vulnerability, and the market data from Q2 2026 is beginning to make this visible. The argument that incumbent enterprise software businesses face existential disruption from AI-native challengers is not wrong in direction, but it is imprecise about magnitude and timing in ways that matter commercially. The strongest incumbents, those with the largest data assets, the most extensive customer relationships, and the deepest integration into enterprise workflows, are not simply defending against AI disruption. They are using AI to accelerate their own position. A significant incumbent can extract financial value directly from AI partnerships, generate commercial growth from AI-native product lines, and position its accumulated data and workflow assets as the most defensible infrastructure layer in the AI era. The transition from seat-based to AI-driven commercial models is not uniformly disadvantageous to large incumbents. It is potentially advantageous to those whose assets are most useful to the AI companies that need them, and it is disruptive to those whose assets are not. The distinction between the two groups is not visible in aggregate market commentary about SaaS disruption. It is visible in specific financial disclosures, in the direction of partnership agreements, and in which incumbents emerge from the transition with stronger competitive positions than they entered it with.[40]

The incumbent acceleration signal

In the second quarter of fiscal year 2027, one of the largest enterprise software platforms reported earnings that produced a stock move of approximately twenty percent in a single session: its second-best day on record. Adjusted earnings per share doubled year on year. Of that earnings figure, $2.53 of $5.90 per share was attributed to investment gains from a strategic AI partnership, with the remainder reflecting underlying commercial growth from strong bookings and a second-half reacceleration in guidance. The company's chief executive publicly dismissed the SaaSpocalypse narrative as "nonsense." Analyst commentary characterised the result as evidence that AI does not replace software but makes the strongest platforms more valuable. The platform's data assets, workflow infrastructure, and customer relationships are not threatened by AI; in the analyst account, they are the input that AI-native commercial models require to function at enterprise scale. The commercial architecture compounds the structural advantage: at the moment of the earnings announcement, the platform was simultaneously embedding a leading AI model in its own product stack and embedding its own sales and workflow capabilities inside that model's native interface. The commercial relationship runs in both directions. That two-way integration is not easily replicated by challengers who hold neither the platform position nor the model partnership, and it is precisely the kind of structural advantage that the transition narrative, in its most sweeping form, underestimates.

The durability signal extends beyond the headline performance of the market's strongest individual players. Population-level revenue data from across the non-AI SaaS cohort complicates the disruption narrative further. The valuation shock of early 2026 removed nearly two trillion dollars from SaaS market capitalisations, reflecting investor anticipation of AI-driven displacement. The revenue data from the same businesses over the same period tells a structurally different story: growth rebounded, with the sharpest recovery among younger companies and in healthcare, retail, and professional services. Equity valuation and commercial revenue are measuring different things. Valuation is a forward-looking instrument that discounts anticipated futures. Payment volumes record what customers are currently paying for and finding worth continuing. The gap between them describes the pace of a transition that investors have already priced and most buyers have not yet completed. The transition narrative is not wrong in direction; it is running one contract cycle ahead of the revenue data that will eventually confirm it.[41]

Revenue holds where valuation fell

Analysis of weekly pay-in volumes across seventy-two thousand non-AI SaaS businesses finds that revenue growth rebounded strongly after the early-2026 valuation selloff. Healthcare, retail, and professional services sectors outperformed pre-crisis trends. Young companies, whose switching costs are lowest and whose buyers are least bound by institutional inertia, showed the strongest recovery. The structural observation that follows is precise: the equity market correctly anticipated a transition; the revenue data correctly recorded that most buyers have not yet completed it. The divergence is not a contradiction. It is the temporal gap between the moment of market perception and the moment of commercial practice, and it implies that incumbents who appear to have more time than the disruption narrative implies may, in fact, have it. The revenue line is not a lagging indicator of the market's health; it is a leading indicator of the transition's pace.

A seventh consideration sits not in the structure of buyer relationships or regulatory constraints but in the revealed commercial behaviour of vendors themselves. Market data from the second quarter of 2026 finds that the large majority of AI agents now visible on commercial pricing pages are bundled into existing plans rather than priced separately or metered on their use. This posture is rational and revealing in equal measure. Rational, because pricing an agent's value requires sufficient deployment data to know what it is worth, and most vendors do not yet possess it at scale. Revealing, because a market in which vendors are actively deferring the pricing decision is preparing to attempt value-based models, not confirming they have arrived. The gap between agents deployed and agents separately priced is the clearest single measure of how early the industry remains in the commercial stages of a transition already well advanced on the product side. The transition narrative and the adoption-before-revenue behaviour are not contradictory. Both can be simultaneously true: the direction is established, and the commercial infrastructure to act on it with confidence is not yet in place for most of the market.[32]
